# Experimental Design Report: Section-by-Section Guide

*What each section of the report form is, what the judges look for, and a strong example of each. Every example comes from the same experiment, so you can see how the sections connect.*

---

## How to Use This Guide

The report form has **13 sections (A–M)** split into two parts. This guide walks through them in order. For each section you will see:

- **What it is** — the purpose of the section in plain language
- **Judges look for** — the exact items on the official scoring checklist
- **Template** — a fill-in-the-blank starting point
- **Strong example** — what a full-credit answer looks like
- **Avoid** — common mistakes that cost points

> **Going deeper:** Statistics, graphing, and CER each have their own guides. This guide gives you the big picture and one complete example of each. Use the **Statistics Reference Guide**, **IQR & Standard Deviation Reference**, and **CER Quick Reference Guide** for the full step-by-step.

### The Sections at a Glance

| Part | Section | Points | Purpose in one line |
|:----:|:--------|:------:|:--------------------|
| **I** | A. Statement of the Problem | 2 | The question your experiment answers |
| **I** | B. Hypothesis | 6 | Your prediction, with a scientific reason |
| **I** | C. Variables | 15 | What you change, what you measure, what you keep the same |
| **I** | D. Materials | 4 | Everything you used, with quantities |
| **I** | E. Procedure and Set-Up Diagrams | 13 | Steps and pictures someone else could follow |
| **I** | F. Qualitative Observations | 6 | What you noticed (without numbers) |
| **I** | G. Quantitative Data | 5 | Your measurements in tables |
| **II** | H. Graph | 12 | A picture of the relationship |
| **II** | I. Statistics | 11 | Numbers that summarize your data |
| **II** | J. Possible Experimental Errors | 6 | What could have gone wrong and how it affects results |
| **II** | K. Analysis (CER) | 18 | Claim, Evidence, Reasoning about trend, outliers, and variation |
| **II** | L. Conclusion | 8 | Was your hypothesis supported? |
| **II** | M. Improvements, Applications, Future Experiments | 12 | What you would do next |

*Points are the State/Nationals totals (118 total). The official checklist shades a few items that are only scored at State/Nationals: the two additional statistics in I, the Variation CER in K, and part of M. Below State, I is worth 9 points, K is worth 12, and M is worth 8, for a 106-point total.*

### The Running Example

**Prompt:** *Design an experiment to investigate how a factor affects how high a ball bounces.*

**Our experiment:** How does the **drop height** of a rubber ball affect the **height of its first bounce**?

Watch how the same **IV (drop height)** and **DV (bounce height)** appear, with the same units, in nearly every section. That consistency is what makes a report feel clear and earns points.

# Part I: Design and Construction of the Experiment

*On the form this part is 20 minutes, but you may keep working on it during Part II.*

---

## A. Statement of the Problem (2 pts)

**What it is:** The one question your experiment will answer. It tells the reader what you are changing and what you are measuring.

**Judges look for:**
- The statement addresses the experiment **including the variables**
- It is **not a yes/no question**

**Template:**

> How does [IV with units] affect [DV with units] of/in [the thing being tested]?

**Strong example:**

> How does the drop height (cm) of a rubber ball affect the height (cm) of its first bounce on a tile floor?

**Avoid:**

| Weak Statement | Why It Loses Points |
|:---------------|:--------------------|
| *Does the drop height change the bounce?* | Yes/no question |
| *What makes a ball bounce higher?* | Does not name a specific IV and DV |
| *Ball bouncing experiment* | Not a question and names no variables |

---

## B. Hypothesis (6 pts)

**What it is:** Your prediction of what will happen, **and why**. It is a prediction you can test with your data, not a guess.

**Judges look for (2 pts each):**
- The statement predicts a **relationship between the IV and DV**
- The statement gives a **specific direction** (increase or decrease)
- A **rationale** (the "because") is given

**Template:**

> If [IV] increases, then [DV] will [increase / decrease], because [scientific reason].

**Strong example:**

> If the drop height of a rubber ball increases, then the height of its first bounce will increase, because a ball released from a greater height has more gravitational potential energy, which becomes more kinetic energy when it hits the floor, leaving more energy to push the ball back up after impact.

**Avoid:**

| Weak Hypothesis | Why It Loses Points |
|:----------------|:--------------------|
| *The ball will bounce.* | No relationship, no direction, no reason |
| *If I drop it higher, it will go higher.* | Vague variables and no "because" |
| *If drop height increases, bounce height will increase because it just will.* | The reason is not scientific |

> **Tip:** The "because" should name a science idea (energy, force, friction, particles moving faster, and so on). Ask yourself, "What is actually happening that would cause this?"

---

## C. Variables (15 pts)

**What it is:** The three kinds of variables in your experiment.

| Type | Meaning | In our example |
|:----:|:--------|:---------------|
| **Independent Variable (IV)** | The one thing **you change** on purpose | Drop height |
| **Dependent Variable (DV)** | The thing **you measure** to see the effect | Bounce height |
| **Controlled Variables (CV)** | Things you **keep the same** so the test is fair | Ball, floor, release method |

**Judges look for:**
- IV correctly identified and **operationally defined** (3 pts)
- **Levels of the IV** given (3 pts)
- DV correctly identified and **operationally defined** (3 pts)
- Three controlled variables, each correctly identified and relevant (2 pts each)

> **Operational definition** = exactly **what** you are changing or measuring, **how** you do it, **with what tool**, and in **what units**. Someone else should be able to repeat it exactly.

**Template:**

> **IV:** [Name]: [how it is changed], measured in [units] with a [tool].
>
> **Levels:** [Level 1], [Level 2], [Level 3]
>
> **DV:** [Name]: [what is measured and when], measured in [units] with a [tool].
>
> **CV:** [Name]: [how it is kept the same].

**Strong example:**

**Independent Variable (IV):** **Drop height** — the vertical distance from the floor to the *bottom of the ball* at the moment of release, measured in **centimeters (cm)** with a **meterstick** taped to the wall.

| Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|:-------:|:-------:|:-------:|
| 20 cm | 40 cm | 60 cm |

**Dependent Variable (DV):** **Bounce height** — the vertical distance from the floor to the *bottom of the ball* at the highest point of its **first** bounce, read to the nearest centimeter from the meterstick (**cm**).

**Controlled Variables (CV):**

| Controlled Variable | How It Is Kept the Same | Why It Matters |
|:--------------------|:------------------------|:---------------|
| Ball | The same rubber ball is used for every trial | A different ball has a different mass and bounciness |
| Floor surface | Every trial is dropped onto the same spot on the same smooth tile floor | Softer surfaces absorb more energy |
| Release method | The ball is held still between two fingers and released by opening them, with no push, spin, or throw | A push would add energy that is not from the drop height |

**Avoid:**

| Mistake | Better |
|:--------|:-------|
| IV: *"height"* (which height?) | *"Drop height of the ball, measured from the floor to the bottom of the ball"* |
| DV: *"how well it bounces"* (not measurable) | *"Height of the first bounce in cm"* |
| Only 2 levels, or levels with no units | Three or more levels, evenly spaced, with units |
| CV: *"everything else"* or *"same room"* | Three specific things that could change the DV if they varied |
| Listing the IV or DV as a controlled variable | The CV list must not include the IV or DV |

## D. Materials (4 pts)

**What it is:** A list of every item you used.

**Judges look for:**
- **All** materials used are listed **and quantified** (2 pts)
- **No unused or extra** materials are listed (2 pts)

**Template:** *[Quantity] [item] ([size or detail if it matters])*

**Strong example:**

- 1 rubber ball (about 6 cm in diameter)
- 1 meterstick (100 cm)
- 1 roll of masking tape
- 1 calculator

> **Tip:** Write the materials list **after** the procedure, then check each step against it. Every item in the list should appear in a step, and every item used in a step should appear in the list. Measuring tools count.

**Avoid:** Missing quantities (*"ball, tape"*), listing items you never used, or forgetting a tool you used (such as the calculator).

---

## E. Procedure and Set-Up Diagrams (13 pts)

**What it is:** A numbered list of steps, plus labeled drawings of your set-up. Another team should be able to repeat your experiment exactly by following it.

**Judges look for:**
- Procedure is in **list form** (2 pts)
- Steps are in a **logical sequence** (2 pts)
- **Steps for repeated trials** are included (2 pts)
- **Multiple diagrams** of the set-up are provided (2 pts)
- All diagrams are **appropriately labeled** (2 pts)
- Procedure is **detailed enough to repeat accurately** (3 pts)

**Strong example — Procedure:**

1. Tape the meterstick **vertically** to a smooth wall, with the 0 cm end touching the tile floor. Press the tape down firmly so the meterstick does not shift.
2. The **dropper** holds the rubber ball between two fingers so that the **bottom of the ball** is level with the **20 cm** mark.
3. The **observer** kneels about 1 m back from the wall, with eyes level with the meterstick.
4. The dropper opens their fingers to release the ball. The ball must not be pushed, spun, or thrown.
5. The observer watches the first bounce and reads the meterstick at the **bottom of the ball at the top of the bounce**. The recorder writes the height in the raw data table (cm).
6. If the ball touches the wall or bounces sideways, **discard** the trial and redo it.
7. Repeat steps 2–6 two more times for a total of **3 trials at 20 cm**.
8. Repeat steps 2–7 for **3 trials at 40 cm**.
9. Repeat steps 2–7 for **3 trials at 60 cm**.
10. Remove the tape, return the materials, and clean up the work area.

**Strong example — Set-Up Diagrams:**

**Diagram 1: Release Heights (Side View)**

```
       wall  meterstick
         |   |
         |   | 60 cm ---- o   Level 3: bottom of ball at 60 cm
         |   |
         |   | 40 cm ---- o   Level 2: bottom of ball at 40 cm
         |   |
         |   | 20 cm ---- o   Level 1: bottom of ball at 20 cm
         |   |
         |   |  0 cm
 ========+===+================ tile floor
```

**Diagram 2: Positions (Top View)**

```
 ================ WALL ================
                [#]  <- meterstick (taped to wall, seen from above)
                 o   <- ball, held beside the meterstick
                (D)  <- Dropper: releases with no push or spin
                 :
                 :   about 1 m
                 :
                (O)  <- Observer: eyes level with the peak,
                        reads the bounce height
```

**Avoid:**

| Mistake | Better |
|:--------|:-------|
| *"Drop the ball and measure."* | Say **how high**, **who** does what, and **how** it is measured |
| Steps written as a paragraph | One action per numbered step |
| *"Do this 3 times"* with no reference | *"Repeat steps 2–6 two more times for 3 trials at 20 cm"* |
| One unlabeled sketch | At least two diagrams, with labels for the IV levels, the tools, and the people |
| No clean-up step | A final step to clean up the work area (an unclean area costs a 0.95 multiplier) |

## F. Qualitative Observations (6 pts)

**What it is:** What you **saw, heard, and felt** while setting up and running the experiment. These are descriptions, **not measurements**. They help explain your results later.

**Judges look for (2 pts each):**
- Observations about the **set-up**
- Observations about the **procedure**
- Observations about the **results**

**Strong example:**

**Set-Up**
- The masking tape held the meterstick well, but the bottom piece peeled slightly after the first few drops, so we pressed it down again before continuing.
- The tile floor was smooth, flat, and clean.

**Procedure**
- Releasing the ball with no spin took practice. The first 20 cm trial had a slight spin, so we redid it.
- At 60 cm, the ball moved quickly and the peak of the bounce was harder to judge by eye.

**Results**
- The ball bounced noticeably higher and made a louder sound as the drop height increased.
- At 60 cm, the ball sometimes bounced slightly sideways instead of straight up.

> **Tip:** Write observations **while** the trials are happening, not afterward. If you wait, you will forget the details.

**Avoid:** *"It went well,"* *"We had fun,"* or numbers (put those in the data table). Be specific about what you actually noticed.

---

## G. Quantitative Data (5 pts)

**What it is:** Your measurements, organized into two tables.

**Judges look for:**
- **Raw data table:** all raw data with **units and labels** (3 pts)
- **Condensed data table:** only the data to be graphed, with **one sample calculation** for each derived value (2 pts)

**Strong example — Raw Data Table**

| Drop Height (cm) | Trial 1 Bounce Height (cm) | Trial 2 Bounce Height (cm) | Trial 3 Bounce Height (cm) |
|:----------------:|:--------------------------:|:--------------------------:|:--------------------------:|
| 20 | 10 | 9 | 11 |
| 40 | 25 | 28 | 22 |
| 60 | 35 | 40 | 30 |

**Strong example — Condensed Data Table**

| Drop Height (cm) | Mean Bounce Height (cm) |
|:----------------:|:-----------------------:|
| 20 | 10.0 |
| 40 | 25.0 |
| 60 | 35.0 |

**Sample calculation (mean at 40 cm):**

$$\bar{x} = \frac{25 + 28 + 22}{3} = \frac{75}{3} = 25.0 \text{ cm}$$

**Avoid:** Missing units, tables with no labels, or a condensed table that includes data you do not graph.

## H. Graph (12 pts)

**What it is:** A picture of how the DV changes as the IV changes. The IV goes on the **x-axis** and the DV goes on the **y-axis**.

**Judges look for (4 pts each):**
- An **appropriate graph** (points for each IV level, with a **line of best fit**, not connect-the-dots)
- Properly **titled and labeled**
- **Appropriate scale and units**

**Graph recipe for our example:**

| Part | What to Write |
|:-----|:--------------|
| **Title** | Effect of Drop Height on Bounce Height of a Rubber Ball |
| **x-axis** | Drop Height (cm), scale 0 to 70 by 10s |
| **y-axis** | Mean Bounce Height (cm), scale 0 to 40 by 5s |
| **Points** | (20, 10), (40, 25), (60, 35) |
| **Line** | One straight line of best fit through the trend |

```
  Mean Bounce Height (cm)
  40 ┤
  35 ┤                                                ×
  30 ┤
  25 ┤                                ×
  20 ┤
  15 ┤
  10 ┤                ×
   5 ┤
   0 ┼────────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───
      0       10      20      30      40      50      60      70
                      Drop Height (cm)

    Title: Effect of Drop Height on Bounce Height of a Rubber Ball
```

*Draw a single straight line of best fit with a ruler so that it follows the overall trend. The points should fall on both sides of the line.*

**Avoid:** Connecting the dots, missing units on the axes, uneven scales, using the IV on the y-axis, or leaving out the title.

## I. Statistics (11 pts; 9 below State)

**What it is:** Numbers that summarize your data for each IV level. **Central tendency** describes the center of the data. **Variation** describes how spread out it is.

**Judges look for:**
- Statistics of **central tendency** used (3 pts)
- **One example calculation** for each, with units, done on one IV level (2 pts)
- Statistics of **variation** included (2 pts)
- **One example calculation** for each, with units (2 pts)
- **Two additional** accurately calculated statistics (2 pts, State/Nationals only)

| Type | Statistics |
|:----:|:-----------|
| **Central tendency** | Mean, median, mode |
| **Variation** | Minimum, maximum, range, IQR, standard deviation, variance |

**Strong example — Results for each IV level**

| Drop Height | Mean (cm) | Median (cm) | Mode (cm) | Min (cm) | Max (cm) | Range (cm) | IQR (cm) | St. Dev (cm) |
|:-----------:|:---------:|:-----------:|:---------:|:--------:|:--------:|:----------:|:--------:|:------------:|
| 20 cm | 10.0 | 10 | none | 9 | 11 | 2 | 2 | 1.0 |
| 40 cm | 25.0 | 25 | none | 22 | 28 | 6 | 6 | 3.0 |
| 60 cm | 35.0 | 35 | none | 30 | 40 | 10 | 10 | 5.0 |

**Strong example — Sample calculations (done on the 40 cm level: 25, 28, 22 cm)**

| Statistic | Calculation | Result |
|:----------|:------------|:------:|
| **Mean** | $(25 + 28 + 22) \div 3$ | 25.0 cm |
| **Median** | Sorted: 22, **25**, 28. The middle value. | 25 cm |
| **Mode** | No value repeats | none |
| **Range** | $28 - 22$ | 6 cm |
| **IQR** | Sorted: 22, 25, 28. $Q1 = 22$, $Q3 = 28$, so $28 - 22$ | 6 cm |
| **Standard deviation** | $\sqrt{\dfrac{(25-25)^2 + (28-25)^2 + (22-25)^2}{3-1}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{18}{2}}$ | 3.0 cm |

> **Always include units.** Every statistic has the same units as the data (cm here). Variance is the exception, since it is in squared units (cm²).

**Avoid:** Leaving off units, skipping the sample calculation, or only calculating statistics for one IV level when the table calls for all of them. See the **Statistics Reference Guide** and **IQR & Standard Deviation Reference** for the full methods.

## J. Possible Experimental Errors (6 pts)

**What it is:** Two **specific** things that could have affected your results, and **how** they would change your data. You also circle the **type** of error for each.

**Judges look for:**
- The **1st specific error** is identified and its **effect on results** is discussed (3 pts)
- The **2nd specific error** is identified and its **effect on results** is discussed (3 pts)

| Error Type | Meaning |
|:----------:|:--------|
| **Random** | Unpredictable small changes from trial to trial. Results are scattered around the true value. |
| **Procedural** | A step was done differently from the plan (a mistake or change in technique). |
| **Systematic** | The same flaw affects **every** trial the same way, shifting all results in one direction. |

**Template:**

> **Specific error identified:** [What exactly happened, and when.]
>
> **Specific error in results:** [Which values were affected, in which direction, and how.]

**Strong example:**

| Error Type | Specific Error Identified | Specific Error in Results |
|:----------:|:--------------------------|:--------------------------|
| **Random** | The observer judged the peak of each bounce by eye. The ball moved quickly, and the observer's eye level changed slightly from trial to trial. | Some readings were a little too high and some a little too low. This scattered the three trials at each height and increased the range and standard deviation, especially at 60 cm where the ball moved fastest. |
| **Systematic** | The meterstick was taped so that its 0 cm mark sat about 1 cm above the floor. | Every drop height and every bounce height was read about 1 cm too low. All data points were shifted by about the same amount, so the trend stayed the same but the means were too low. |

**Avoid:**

| Weak Error | Why It Loses Points |
|:-----------|:--------------------|
| *"Human error"* or *"We made mistakes"* | Not specific |
| *"The ball was bad"* | No specific error and no effect on the results |
| *"This made our data wrong"* | Does not say **how** the data changed (higher, lower, more spread out?) |

## K. Analysis of Claim / Evidence / Reasoning (18 pts; 12 below State)

**What it is:** A structured way to explain what your data shows. You write one CER for each of three questions: the **data trend**, **outliers**, and **variation**.

| Part | What It Is | Remember |
|:----:|:-----------|:---------|
| **Claim** | A statement that answers the question | A sentence, no numbers needed |
| **Evidence** | The data that backs up the claim | Numbers from your tables, graph, or statistics |
| **Reasoning** | Explains **why** the evidence supports the claim | Links the evidence back to the claim ("Since... therefore...") |

**Judges look for:** Each claim, evidence, and reasoning (2 pts each) is **completed logically**.

> **Going deeper:** The **CER Quick Reference Guide** has fill-in-the-blank templates for each of these, including how to find the line of best fit and the outlier bounds.

### Data Trend

**Claim:** There is a **positive correlation** between bounce height and drop height.

**Evidence:** The line of best fit for the mean bounce height plotted against drop height is $y = 0.63x - 1.7$.

**Reasoning:** Since the slope of the line of best fit is 0.63, which is positive, the bounce height increases as the drop height increases. Therefore, there is a positive correlation between bounce height and drop height.

### Outliers

**Claim:** There are **no outliers** in the data.

**Evidence:** Using the 1.5 × IQR rule ($\text{Lower} = Q1 - 1.5 \times IQR$, $\text{Upper} = Q3 + 1.5 \times IQR$):

| Drop Height | Lower Bound (cm) | Upper Bound (cm) |
|:-----------:|:----------------:|:----------------:|
| 20 cm | 6 | 14 |
| 40 cm | 13 | 37 |
| 60 cm | 15 | 55 |

**Reasoning:** Since every data point falls between the lower and upper bounds for its drop height (for example, the largest 20 cm trial was 11 cm, which is below the upper bound of 14 cm), there are no outliers based on the 1.5 × IQR rule.

### Variation *(State/Nationals)*

**Claim:** As the drop height increased, the variation in bounce height **increased**.

**Evidence:** The standard deviation was 1.0 cm at 20 cm, 3.0 cm at 40 cm, and 5.0 cm at 60 cm.

**Reasoning:** The standard deviation increases as the drop height increases. Since standard deviation shows how far data points typically are from the mean, this means the trials were more spread out at greater drop heights. Therefore, the increase in drop height increased the variability of the bounce heights.

**Avoid:**

| Mistake | Better |
|:--------|:-------|
| Putting numbers in the **claim** only | Claim = the statement. Numbers belong in the **evidence**. |
| Evidence with no numbers (*"the data goes up"*) | Cite specific values: the slope, the standard deviations, the bounds |
| Reasoning that just repeats the claim | Explain **why** the evidence proves the claim (*"Since... therefore..."*) |

## L. Conclusion (8 pts)

**What it is:** Your final answer to the question. Did the data support your hypothesis? It is a CER about your **hypothesis**.

**Judges look for (2 pts each):**
- The **hypothesis is restated**
- The hypothesis **claim** (accept or reject) is completed logically
- The **evidence** is completed logically
- The **reasoning** is completed logically

**Template:**

> **Hypothesis restated:** If [IV] increases, then [DV] will [increase / decrease], because [reason].
>
> **Claim:** The hypothesis was [accepted (supported) / rejected (not supported)].
>
> **Evidence:** [Data from the condensed table and the line of best fit.]
>
> **Reasoning:** Since [data pattern], the data [supports / does not support] the hypothesis. This makes sense because [science explanation].

**Strong example:**

**Hypothesis restated:** If the drop height of a rubber ball increases, then the height of its first bounce will increase, because a ball released from a greater height has more gravitational potential energy, which becomes more kinetic energy when it hits the floor, leaving more energy to push the ball back up after impact.

**Claim:** The hypothesis was **accepted (supported)**. As the drop height increased, the bounce height increased.

**Evidence:** The mean bounce height was 10.0 cm at a drop height of 20 cm, 25.0 cm at 40 cm, and 35.0 cm at 60 cm. The line of best fit, $y = 0.63x - 1.7$, has a positive slope.

**Reasoning:** Since the mean bounce height increased every time the drop height increased, and the line of best fit has a positive slope, the data support the hypothesis. This makes sense because a ball dropped from higher up has more gravitational potential energy to convert into kinetic energy. Some of that energy is lost as sound and heat when the ball hits the floor, but a higher drop leaves more energy to lift the ball back up.

> **If your data does not support your hypothesis:** That is completely fine. Say the hypothesis was **rejected**, cite the data that shows it, and explain what may have caused the difference (your experimental errors from Section J are a good place to look). You are graded on how well you use your data, not on being right.

**Avoid:** Saying the data **"proved"** the hypothesis, forgetting to restate the hypothesis, or giving evidence that does not match your tables and graph.

## M. Improvements, Applications, and Further Use (12 pts; 8 below State)

**What it is:** Looking beyond this one experiment: how to make it better, where the science is used, and what to test next.

**Judges look for (4 pts each):**
- **Suggestions to improve** the experiment, **with rationale**
- Suggestions for **practical applications**
- Suggestions for **future experiments**

**Strong example:**

**Improvements for the Current Experiment**
- Record each bounce with a phone camera in slow motion, placed next to the meterstick, and read the peak frame by frame. This would reduce the observer's reading error (a random error), which would lower the range and standard deviation, especially at 60 cm.
- Use 5 trials per drop height instead of 3. More trials make the mean less affected by one unusual trial and give a more reliable line of best fit.

**Practical Applications**
- **Sports equipment:** Manufacturers test balls by dropping them from a set height and measuring the rebound. This makes sure every ball plays the same way.
- **Safety surfaces:** Playground and gym floors are designed to absorb energy rather than return it, so falls are safer. Knowing how drop height affects energy return helps engineers choose the right materials.

**Future Experiments**
- Test how the **type of surface** (tile, carpet, wood, foam) affects the bounce height at a constant drop height.
- Test whether the bounce height keeps rising at the same rate at **greater drop heights** (80, 100, and 120 cm).
- Test how the **type of ball** (rubber, tennis, foam) affects the bounce height at a constant drop height.

> **Tip:** Every improvement needs a **because** (a rationale), and every future experiment should name a new IV and DV. The **Experiment Applications Reference** guide has more ideas.

**Avoid:** *"Do more trials"* with no reason, applications that have nothing to do with your science, and future experiments that repeat what you already did.

# Putting It All Together

## Check That Your Report Tells One Story

The same IV and DV, with the same units, should appear in every section. Use this chart to check your own report.

| Section | What to Check |
|:-------:|:--------------|
| **A** | Names the IV and DV with units |
| **B** | Predicts how the DV changes as the IV changes, and says why |
| **C** | Operationally defines the IV, the DV, and three CVs; lists three levels |
| **D–E** | Materials match the procedure; the procedure uses the exact IV levels |
| **G** | Table headings use the same variable names and units |
| **H** | IV on the x-axis, DV on the y-axis, same units |
| **I–K** | Statistics, outlier bounds, and slope all use data from your tables |
| **L** | Restates the hypothesis from **B** and uses evidence from **G–K** |
| **M** | Improvements connect to the errors in **J** |

## Tiebreakers and Multipliers

If two teams have the same score, ties are broken in this order:

1. **K:** CER
2. **E:** Procedure
3. **C:** Variables
4. **G:** Data Table
5. **H:** Graph

Your total score can also be reduced by a **multiplier**:

| Multiplier | Reason |
|:----------:|:-------|
| **0.95** | Materials were left out or the work area was not cleaned up |
| **Up to 0.75** | Off topic |
| **0.25** | Non-lab, fake, or falsified data |

## Final Checklist

- The problem is a question that names the IV and DV (not yes/no)
- The hypothesis has a direction **and** a "because"
- The IV and DV are operationally defined with units and tools
- Three IV levels and three controlled variables are listed
- The materials are quantified and match the procedure
- The procedure is numbered, includes repeated trials, and has two or more labeled diagrams
- Observations cover the set-up, procedure, **and** results
- Raw and condensed data tables have units and labels
- The graph has a title, labeled axes with units, a scale, and a line of best fit
- Statistics include sample calculations with units
- Two specific errors describe **how** they changed the results
- Every CER has a claim, evidence (with numbers), and reasoning
