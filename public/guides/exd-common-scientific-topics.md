# Common Scientific Topics for Experimental Design

*A quick-reference guide of topics frequently seen at Science Olympiad Experimental Design (Division B). Use this to brainstorm hypotheses, identify variables, and recall the science behind expected results.*

---

## How to Use This Guide

During the event you will be given materials and a general topic area. Recognizing the underlying science **before** you sit down helps you:

1. **Choose strong IV / DV pairs** — pick variables that produce a clear, measurable trend.
2. **Predict the correlation** — a solid hypothesis backed by a formula or principle earns more points.
3. **Explain your reasoning** — judges reward concise scientific explanations in your CER sections.

Each row below lists a topic, one or two suggested IV / DV combinations, the expected correlation, and a brief scientific explanation.

---

## Physics Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 1 | **Ramp Height & Rolling Distance** | Height of the ramp (cm) | Distance the ball rolls after leaving the ramp (cm) | **Positive** — greater height → greater distance | A higher ramp gives the ball more gravitational potential energy ($PE = mgh$), which converts to kinetic energy ($KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$). More speed at the bottom means it rolls farther. |
| 2 | **Pendulum Length & Period** | Length of the pendulum string (cm) | Time for one full swing — period (s) | **Positive** — longer string → longer period | Period increases with length: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{L}{g}}$. A longer string traces a bigger arc, so each swing takes more time. |
| 3 | **Mass on a Spring & Stretch Distance** | Mass hung on the spring (g) | Distance the spring stretches (cm) | **Positive** — more mass → more stretch | Hooke's Law: $F = kx$. Since $F = mg$, adding mass increases force and therefore extension ($x = \tfrac{mg}{k}$). |
| 4 | **Projectile Launch Angle & Horizontal Distance** | Launch angle of a projectile (°) | Horizontal distance traveled (cm) | **Positive up to 45°, then negative** — distance peaks near 45° | Range formula: $R = \tfrac{v^{2}\sin 2\theta}{g}$. The $\sin 2\theta$ term is maximized at $\theta = 45°$. |
| 5 | **Surface Type & Friction (Sliding Distance)** | Type of surface (e.g., sandpaper, wood, tile) | Distance an object slides before stopping (cm) | **Varies by surface** — rougher surface → shorter slide distance | Friction force $f = \mu N$ opposes motion. Surfaces with a higher coefficient of friction ($\mu$) slow objects faster, reducing slide distance. |
| 6 | **Ramp Angle & Acceleration** | Angle of the ramp (°) | Time for an object to travel a fixed distance down the ramp (s) | **Negative** — steeper angle → shorter time (greater acceleration) | The component of gravity along the ramp is $a = g\sin\theta$. A steeper angle increases this component, so the object accelerates faster. |

## Forces & Motion Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 7 | **Parachute Canopy Size & Fall Time** | Diameter of the parachute canopy (cm) | Time to fall a set height (s) | **Positive** — larger canopy → longer fall time | A larger canopy increases air resistance (drag), which opposes gravity and slows the fall. Drag force increases with cross-sectional area. |
| 8 | **Paper Airplane Wing Span & Flight Distance** | Wing span of a paper airplane (cm) | Horizontal flight distance (cm) | **Positive (up to a point)** — wider wings → greater distance, but diminishing returns if too wide | Wider wings generate more lift by deflecting more air downward. However, very large wings add mass and drag, eventually reducing performance. |
| 9 | **Rubber Band Stretch & Launch Distance** | Distance a rubber band is pulled back (cm) | Distance the projectile travels (cm) | **Positive** — greater stretch → greater launch distance | The rubber band stores elastic potential energy ($PE_{e} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^{2}$). More stretch stores more energy, which converts to kinetic energy upon release. |
| 10 | **Added Mass & Rolling Speed** | Mass added to a wheeled cart (g) | Time to roll a fixed distance down a ramp (s) | **Roughly constant** — mass has little effect on acceleration down a frictionless ramp | On an ideal ramp, $a = g\sin\theta$ is independent of mass. In practice, added mass slightly increases friction, so time may increase slightly. |

## Heat & Energy Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 11 | **Water Temperature & Cooling Rate** | Starting water temperature (°C) | Temperature drop over a fixed time interval (°C) | **Positive** — hotter water → faster temperature drop | Newton's Law of Cooling: rate of heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference between the object and its surroundings ($\tfrac{dT}{dt} \propto -(T - T_{env})$). |
| 12 | **Insulation Material & Heat Retention** | Type of insulating material (foam, cloth, foil, none) | Water temperature after a set time (°C) | **Varies by material** — better insulators → higher remaining temperature | Insulation reduces the rate of conductive and convective heat transfer. Materials with lower thermal conductivity (e.g., foam) retain heat longer. |
| 13 | **Cup Material & Cooling Rate** | Material of the cup (plastic, metal, paper, styrofoam) | Time for water to cool by 10 °C (min) | **Varies by material** — lower thermal conductivity → slower cooling | Heat transfers through the cup wall by conduction ($Q = \tfrac{kA\Delta T}{d}$). Metal has high $k$ (cools fast); styrofoam has low $k$ (cools slowly). |
| 14 | **Water Volume & Heating Time** | Volume of water (mL) | Time to reach a target temperature (s) | **Positive** — more water → longer heating time | Heat energy required: $Q = mc\Delta T$. More mass ($m$) requires more energy at the same power input, so it takes longer. |

## Chemistry & Dissolving Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 15 | **Water Temperature & Dissolving Time** | Temperature of the water (°C) | Time for a sugar cube to fully dissolve (s) | **Negative** — hotter water → faster dissolving | Higher temperature increases molecular kinetic energy, causing water molecules to collide with the solute more frequently and break it apart faster. |
| 16 | **Particle Size & Dissolving Rate** | Grain size of sugar (powdered, granulated, cube) | Time to dissolve completely (s) | **Negative** — smaller particles → shorter dissolving time | Smaller particles have a greater surface-area-to-volume ratio, exposing more solute to the solvent and speeding dissolution. |
| 17 | **Vinegar Amount & Gas Production** | Volume of vinegar added to baking soda (mL) | Height of foam produced (cm) | **Positive (up to a limit)** — more vinegar → more foam, until baking soda is fully reacted | The reaction $\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{NaCH}_3\text{COO}$ produces $\text{CO}_2$ gas. More acid reacts with more baking soda, generating more gas until the limiting reagent is consumed. |
| 18 | **Salt Concentration & Freezing Point** | Amount of salt dissolved in water (g) | Temperature at which the solution freezes (°C) | **Negative** — more salt → lower freezing point | Dissolved solute particles interfere with ice crystal formation (freezing-point depression): $\Delta T_f = K_f \cdot m \cdot i$, where $m$ is molality and $i$ is the van 't Hoff factor. |

## Water & Fluid Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 19 | **Salt Concentration & Buoyancy** | Amount of salt dissolved in water (g/L) | Depth to which an object sinks (cm), or number of objects that float | **Negative for sinking depth** — more salt → object sinks less (floats higher) | Adding salt increases water density. An object floats when its weight equals the weight of displaced fluid ($F_b = \rho_{fluid} \cdot V \cdot g$). Denser fluid requires less displaced volume to support the same object. |
| 20 | **Soap Concentration & Surface Tension** | Drops of dish soap added to water | Number of water drops that fit on a penny | **Negative** — more soap → fewer drops fit on the penny | Soap is a surfactant that reduces surface tension by disrupting hydrogen bonds between water molecules. Lower surface tension means the water dome on the penny breaks sooner. |
| 21 | **Water Temperature & Viscosity** | Temperature of the liquid (°C) | Time for a marble to sink to the bottom of a container (s) | **Negative** — warmer liquid → shorter sink time (lower viscosity) | Higher temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, reducing intermolecular attractions and lowering the fluid's viscosity. The marble experiences less drag and sinks faster. |
| 22 | **Container Shape & Evaporation Rate** | Surface area of the container opening (cm²) | Mass of water lost over a fixed time (g) | **Positive** — larger opening → more evaporation | Evaporation occurs at the liquid surface. A greater exposed surface area allows more molecules to escape into the air per unit time. |

## Magnetism & Electricity Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 23 | **Distance & Magnetic Force** | Distance between a magnet and a metal object (cm) | Number of paper clips the magnet can pick up, or force measured (N) | **Negative** — greater distance → weaker magnetic attraction | Magnetic field strength decreases rapidly with distance (roughly $B \propto \tfrac{1}{d^{3}}$ for a dipole). Fewer clips can be lifted as distance increases. |
| 24 | **Number of Coils & Electromagnet Strength** | Number of wire coils wrapped around an iron nail | Number of paper clips picked up | **Positive** — more coils → stronger electromagnet | Each coil of wire carrying current adds to the magnetic field: $B = \mu_0 \cdot n \cdot I$, where $n$ is the number of turns per unit length. More turns concentrate the field. |

## Sound Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 25 | **Rubber Band Length & Pitch** | Vibrating length of a rubber band (cm) | Pitch / frequency of the sound produced (Hz) | **Negative** — longer vibrating length → lower pitch | Frequency of a vibrating string: $f = \tfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\tfrac{T}{\mu}}$. Increasing length $L$ lowers frequency, producing a deeper sound. |
| 26 | **Water Level in a Bottle & Pitch** | Volume of water in a glass bottle (mL) | Pitch produced when the bottle rim is struck (Hz) | **Negative** — more water → lower pitch when struck | When struck, the glass and water vibrate together. More water adds mass, lowering the natural frequency. (Note: when *blowing* across the top, the trend reverses because the air column shortens.) |

## Biology & Life Science Topics

| # | Topic | Independent Variable (IV) | Dependent Variable (DV) | Expected Correlation | Scientific Explanation |
|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| 27 | **Light Exposure & Plant Growth** | Hours of light per day (h) | Height of the plant after a set number of days (cm) | **Positive (up to a point)** — more light → more growth, plateauing at saturation | Plants use light energy for photosynthesis ($6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$). More light drives more sugar production for growth, until other factors become limiting. |
| 28 | **Fertilizer Concentration & Seed Germination** | Concentration of liquid fertilizer (mL/L) | Percentage of seeds germinated after 7 days (%) | **Positive then negative** — moderate fertilizer helps; too much inhibits germination | Nutrients support early root growth, but excess fertilizer raises solute concentration, creating osmotic stress that pulls water away from the seed. |

|:-:|:------|:--------------------------|:------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|

## Tips for State-Level Success

- **Know the science first.** Judges look for correct scientific reasoning in your hypothesis and CER sections. Memorizing the formulas and principles above will help you write a strong hypothesis quickly.
- **Pick measurable DVs.** Whenever possible choose a DV you can measure with a number and a unit (e.g., cm, s, g, °C) rather than a subjective observation.
- **Expect the unexpected.** At the state level the materials or topic may be unfamiliar. Use the categories above to connect an unfamiliar prompt to a known science principle.
- **Control your constants.** For every IV / DV pair listed, think about what must stay the same (constants) to make the test fair.
- **Use at least 3 IV levels with 3 trials each.** This gives you enough data to calculate meaningful statistics (mean, standard deviation, IQR) and draw a proper line of best fit.
